ST310 course page

A core set of commonly used machine learning tools, practiced in R, with attention to their mathematical foundations, their statistical limitations, and what they do and do not tell you.

Readings and lectures are complementary so do both, and bring a laptop with R and RStudio to the seminar (see about for setup). The seminar notebook for each week is linked below; the .qmd link downloads the source file to open in RStudio, the other link is the same notebook rendered so you can read it in a browser. Click a week’s heading, or its entry in the contents, to open it.

Week 1: prediction competitions and nearest neighbors

What machine learning is and where it came from: statistics and computer science, and the prediction competition (the common task framework) as its working definition, with the Netflix prize as the cautionary tale. A first prediction task, the rule that a prediction may use only what is available at prediction time, a held-out set as the score, and \(k\)-nearest neighbors as the first method. The bias–variance decomposition derived for \(k\)-NN at the board, and why nearest neighbors stop working in many dimensions.

Reading

  • ISLR Chapter 1 (Introduction), pp. 1–14. A quick read; don’t skip the section on notation and simple matrix algebra.
  • ISLR Chapter 2 (Statistical Learning), sections 2.1 and 2.2, pp. 15–42. If R is new to you, also do the lab in section 2.3, pp. 42–52.
  • PPA Chapter 1 (Introduction), pp. 1–10.

If you finish these quickly you may want to start Chapter 3, because it’s a long one.

Week 2: linear regression

Regression as the first fitted model rather than a review: least squares and the hat matrix, and the conditional mean as the target that every regression method is aiming at (the optimal model theorem). Multiple regression and what a coefficient holds fixed: the partialling-out theorem, proved in the lecture and reused later in the course, and omitted-variable bias, what the same algebra says when a relevant column is left out.

Reading

  • ISLR Chapter 3 (Linear Regression), sections 3.1–3.3 and 3.5, pp. 61–110. Mostly review from your regression course; read 3.2.1 (estimating the coefficients in multiple regression) and 3.3.3 (potential problems) slowly. The lab in 3.6 goes with the seminar.
  • PPA Chapter 2 (Fundamentals of prediction), the sections “Modeling knowledge” and “Prediction via optimization”, pp. 13–21.

Optional, deeper: ESL section 2.4 (the optimal model theorem) and section 3.2, pp. 44–57 (3.2.3 is the partialling-out theorem).

Week 3: classification, and causality (part 1)

Classification: logistic regression, a threshold and the two kinds of error on either side of it, and the metrics that follow; the optimal threshold theorem says where the threshold goes, given the costs. Causality, part 1: prediction against intervention, a world in which the outcome causes the predictor, and why a model that predicts well can still give the wrong answer to a what-if question. The adjustment theorem says when averaging over a measured variable recovers the effect of an intervention, and the inverse propensity weighting theorem, proved in the lecture, gets the same effect by weighting with the probability of treatment, a class probability that logistic regression estimates.

Reading

  • ISLR Chapter 4 (Classification), sections 4.1–4.3, pp. 130–141. Section 4.4 (LDA, QDA, naive Bayes) is not part of this course; skim 4.5, pp. 158–164, for the comparison with \(k\)-nearest neighbors.
  • PPA Chapter 9 (Causality), from “The limitations of observation” through “Confounding”, pp. 179–192.

Optional: PPA Chapter 2 (Fundamentals of prediction), “Types of errors and successes” and “The Neyman–Pearson lemma”, pp. 21–26, and “Decisions that discriminate”, pp. 26–31.

Week 4: optimization and model fitting

How a model actually gets fitted. Logistic regression first: maximum likelihood, the score equations read as orthogonality, like the normal equations, and why there is no closed form. Then the method that fits almost everything else: gradient descent, step sizes and the descent lemma, and its stochastic version, whose random steps are unbiased for the full gradient. Empirical risk minimization as the common form of every method so far, the bias–variance decomposition in that form, why no method is best on every problem, and early stopping as a first kind of regularization.

Reading

  • PPA Chapter 5 (Optimization), from “Optimization basics” through “The SGD quick start guide”, pp. 70–86, stopping at “Analysis of the stochastic gradient method”: gradient descent, the quadratic case, and stochastic gradient descent in practice.

Optional: PPA “Analysis of the stochastic gradient method”, pp. 86–88 (a convergence bound), and “Regularization” with “Implicit regularization by optimization”, pp. 92–94 (why gradient descent from zero lands on the minimum-norm solution, which the seminar meets); ESL section 4.4.1 for Newton-IRLS in full; the Wikipedia pages on Newton’s method and gradient descent for pictures and animations.

Lecture: slides

Seminar: notebook (.qmd) · notebook with solutions. You write gradient descent once, as a function, and use it for least squares, logistic regression and random batches. Data: gapminder and the Palmer penguins (data note), then simulated data.

Week 5: functional structures

Five ways to shape a predictor, introduced together and compared on one task: ridge and the lasso (what a penalty changes, and what \(\lambda\) controls), additive models (a spline as regression on basis functions, one curve per input), trees and random forests, and a neural network as a GLM of GLMs. Each has a knob, tuned on held-out data, with a test set opened once. Simulated data with three kinds of truth show that no structure wins on all three, and the optimism theorem says why training error underestimates the risk of a fitted model. Data: Spotify tracks, what makes a song popular.

Reading

  • ISLR Chapter 6 (Linear Model Selection and Regularization), sections 6.2.1–6.2.2, pp. 237–250: ridge regression and the lasso, and what each penalty does to the coefficients. Stop before 6.2.3.
  • ISLR Chapter 7 (Moving Beyond Linearity), sections 7.3–7.4, pp. 294–301 (basis functions and regression splines), and section 7.7, pp. 306–310 (generalized additive models; 7.7.1 fits one by least squares on spline basis functions, as the lecture does, and the GAM part of the lab in 7.8 has the lm and ns() code). Skim 7.6, pp. 304–306 (local regression).
  • ISLR Chapter 8 (Tree-Based Methods), section 8.1.1, pp. 328–335 (regression trees), and sections 8.2.1–8.2.2, pp. 340–345 (bagging and random forests). Skim 8.1.2–8.1.4, pp. 335–340.

Optional: PPA Chapter 6 (Generalization), “Generalization gap”, pp. 100–101, the setting of the optimism theorem.

Week 6: reading week

No lecture or seminar. Start week 7’s reading: ISLR section 5.1, pp. 197–209 (cross-validation), which week 7 opens with.

Week 7: tuning and regularization

Every method so far had a knob. This week: choosing it by \(K\)-fold cross-validation, with the curve it draws against \(\lambda\), \(k\) and tree depth, and the standard error of a held-out score. Then how the error depends on \(n\) and the number of inputs: from the bias–variance decomposition, the error of \(k\)-nearest neighbors with \(p\) inputs, the rate \(n^{-2/(p+2)}\), and the faster rates an additive or a sparse truth allows. Ridge and the lasso side by side in the simplest design: shrinkage against soft thresholding, and selection against prediction; the smoothing penalty on a spline as ridge with weights. Double descent: which zero-error fit you get past the interpolation threshold, why the test error can fall again, and why that does not contradict the bias–variance trade-off.

Reading

  • ISLR Chapter 5 (Resampling Methods), section 5.1, pp. 198–208: the validation set, leave-one-out and \(K\)-fold cross-validation, and the bias–variance trade-off in choosing \(K\). Skim 5.1.5 (classification), pp. 206–208. Section 5.2, the bootstrap, is not needed this week.
  • ISLR Chapter 6, section 6.2.3, pp. 250–251 (choosing the tuning parameter by cross-validation), picking up where the last reading stopped. Re-read “A simple special case for ridge regression and the lasso”, pp. 247–249: the lecture works in that design.
  • ISLR Chapter 6, section 6.4, pp. 261–267 (what changes when \(p > n\)). Section 6.3, which comes between them, is not part of the course.
  • ISLR Chapter 7, section 7.5.1, pp. 301–302 (smoothing splines: a penalty on wiggliness instead of a basis size). Skim 7.5.2, pp. 302–304.

Optional: PPA Chapter 6 (Generalization), “Generalization gap” and “Overparameterization: empirical phenomena”, pp. 100–104 (the double-descent curve, and why it is not universal); ISLR section 10.8, pp. 439–443 (interpolation and double descent, with the natural-spline example the lecture demonstrates); ISLR section 3.5, pp. 105–110, re-read (what happens to \(k\)-nearest neighbors as the number of inputs grows).

Deeper: ESL section 7.10.2, pp. 245–247 (the wrong and the right way to do cross-validation, which the seminar meets); ESL section 2.5, pp. 22–28 (local methods in high dimensions); ESL sections 3.4.1–3.4.3, pp. 61–73 (ridge and the lasso in general designs); Belkin, M., Hsu, D., Ma, S. and Mandal, S. Week 8: additivity vs interactions, and ensembles

How some flexible fits are built. An additive model fitted one curve at a time, each to what the others leave; its one-input plots, which are partial dependence plots; how additivity can be importantly wrong when the truth has interactions, and how a correct additive fit can still answer the wrong causal question. A tree built one greedy split at a time, then pruned. Bagging and random forests: the variance of an average of correlated trees, and the out-of-bag error. Boosting as approximate gradient descent, one small tree at a time. Partial dependence for other models, and feature importance, come later in the course. Data: IMDb films (data note).

Reading

  • ISLR Chapter 8 (Tree-Based Methods), section 8.1, pp. 327–340, all of it this time: regression trees again, pruning, classification trees, trees against linear models.
  • ISLR Chapter 8, sections 8.2.1–8.2.3, pp. 340–348: bagging (with out-of-bag error and variable importance measures), random forests, boosting. Section 8.2.4, Bayesian additive regression trees, is not part of the course.
  • ISLR Chapter 7, section 7.7.1, pp. 307–310, re-read: fitting an additive model one predictor at a time (backfitting).

Optional, deeper: ESL section 9.1.1, pp. 297–299 (the backfitting algorithm); sections 10.10–10.12 (gradient boosting, tree size, regularization).

The seminar’s problems ask how far a tuning score can be from the error it estimates, and what a larger search costs. Then tuning a random forest and boosted trees on the films: one knob at a time against all of them together, how the size of the search changes what the best score means, and the test films opened once.

Week 9: uncertainty and interpretation

What a fitted prediction leaves out, and what it can tell us. The loss decides which number is the best prediction: the mean, the median, a cost threshold, or a quantile. A prediction interval for one person’s outcome, from residuals or from two quantiles: how to check one on fresh data, overall and across the range of the inputs, how bias and variance break intervals computed on the wrong rows, how residuals from held-out rows guarantee coverage on average over the inputs, what coverage at every input needs as the sample grows, and why no method can guarantee it without assumptions. Calibration: when a stated probability matches a frequency, what that means for decisions, what it does not say about a score, how to repair one, and calibration within groups, a fairness criterion. Then interpretation: partial dependence and individual curves for any model, Shapley values, and permutation and leave-one-covariate-out importance. Last, the model and the world: a causal model for the inputs, the difference between what a partial dependence plot holds fixed and the total dependence of a prediction on an input, and when a model’s curve is an effect.

Reading

  • Prediction intervals and calibration (PDF), a note for this lecture: what an interval or a probability claims, and how to check it on fresh data.
  • IML, the chapters “Partial Dependence Plot (PDP)”, “Individual Conditional Expectation (ICE)” and “Permutation Feature Importance”: the definitions, the examples and the limitations.

Optional: Loftus, J. R., Bynum, L. E. J. and Hansen, S. (2024), “Causal dependence plots”, Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 37, doi:10.52202/079017-3579, section 1 with its Figure 1; Zhao, Q. and Hastie, T. (2021), “Causal interpretations of black-box models”, Journal of Business and Economic Statistics 39(1), 272–281, doi:10.1080/07350015.2019.1624293.

Week 10: deployment and evaluation

What a winning score shows, and what it does not. The leaderboard first: comparing two models on the same rows, paired; Hoeffding’s inequality and the union bound, which say how far every score can be from its risk at once; choosing the comparison before the test rows are opened; the bootstrap for numbers that are not averages; and scores that drift when the candidates are tuned to them. Then the data move: in-distribution against out-of-distribution generalization, covariate shift and reweighting by a ratio of probabilities, label shift and a change of intercept, and what each shift does to accuracy, calibration, and ranking. Last, scores and decisions: curve fitting with and without a theory behind it, what a competition win does and does not license, a cost score used to allocate extra care that is accurate and calibrated and still allocates it unequally, and prediction tasks that a better score would not make better. Data: synthetic patient-years from a study of a commercial health algorithm (data note).

Reading [PROVISIONAL]

  • You found the best predictor. Now what? (PDF), the note for this lecture: competitions and benchmarks, how far a score can be from its risk, distribution shift, and what a winning score cannot justify. The works it draws on are listed in each section and at the end.

Week 11: what changes when the representation is learned?

Every method in this course was a predictor on features someone chose. What changes when the features are built by hand (week 8’s bases and kernels), learned together with the task (a network with one hidden layer as a basis the data chooses, fitted by week 4’s stochastic gradient descent; everything beyond that is ST311’s), or transferred from a model trained on something else (embeddings, with week 1’s nearest neighbors and week 5’s ridge run on them). Then the course’s frame read again with everything since: what the held-out leaderboard delivered, and where it stops, each limit being a result from the term. The theorems in one view, as a reference sheet.

Reading [PROVISIONAL]

  • PPA Chapter 4 (Representations and features), pp. 49–67: measurement, and “Nonlinear predictors” (polynomials, kernels and random features, and a neural network as a learned representation).
  • ISLR Chapter 10, sections 10.1–10.2, pp. 404–411 (single- and multilayer neural networks), for the one network the lecture builds. The rest of Chapter 10 is ST311’s.

Delivered in lecture, for reference rather than preparation:

  • Rodu and Baiocchi, “When black box algorithms are (not) appropriate”, Observational Studies 9(2), 2023 (arXiv 2001.07648): the common task framework and what it does and does not establish.
  • Wang, Kapoor, Barocas and Narayanan, “Against predictive optimization”, ACM Journal on Responsible Computing 1(1), 2024.

Optional: ISLR Chapter 12, section 12.2, from p. 498 (principal components, the simplest learned representation); Belkin, Hsu, Ma and Mandal, PNAS 2019, if not read in week 7.
