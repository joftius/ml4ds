Before the seminar

The lecture stated two results and left their proofs to you (exercises 1 and 2), and proved a third, which you redo with the slides closed (exercise 3). Do all three on paper before the seminar; the seminar’s problems are variations on the first two. You may use, without proof, two facts about conditional expectation from ST102: the tower property, \(\mathbb E[Z] = \mathbb E\big[\mathbb E[Z \mid X]\big]\), and taking out what is known, \(\mathbb E[g(X)\, Z \mid X] = g(X)\, \mathbb E[Z \mid X]\) for any function \(g\) of \(X\) (given \(X\), a function of \(X\) is a constant).

WarningHints

Each exercise has hints, collapsed below it. Do not open a hint unless you have been stuck for at least a few minutes, and open them in order. The solutions are revealed in the seminar.

Exercise 1 (optimal model theorem). Let \(\mathbb E Y^2 < \infty\), \(f^\star(x) = \mathbb E[Y \mid X = x]\) and \(R(f) = \mathbb E\big[(Y - f(X))^2\big]\). Show that for every \(f\) with \(\mathbb E f(X)^2 < \infty\), \[ R(f) = R(f^\star) + \mathbb E\big[(f^\star(X) - f(X))^2\big]. \] Conclude that \(f^\star\) has the smallest risk of all functions of \(X\), and that the excess risk of any \(f\), fitted by any method, is the one number \(\mathbb E[(f^\star(X) - f(X))^2]\).

NoteHint 1

Add and subtract \(f^\star(X)\) inside the square, then expand.

NoteHint 2

Show that the cross term, \(\mathbb E\big[(Y - f^\star(X))\,(f^\star(X) - f(X))\big]\), is zero.

NoteHint 3

Condition on \(X\) first (the tower property), then take out what is known.

TipReveal solution

Hints 1 to 3 in order. Expanding \(Y - f(X) = \big(Y - f^\star(X)\big) + \big(f^\star(X) - f(X)\big)\): \[ R(f) = R(f^\star) + \mathbb E\big[(f^\star(X) - f(X))^2\big] + 2\,\mathbb E\big[(Y - f^\star(X))\,(f^\star(X) - f(X))\big]. \] The cross term vanishes because \(g(X) = f^\star(X) - f(X)\) is a function of \(X\): \[ \mathbb E\big[(Y - f^\star(X))\,g(X)\big] = \mathbb E\Big[g(X)\,\mathbb E\big[Y - f^\star(X) \mid X\big]\Big] = 0, \] by the tower property, then taking out what is known, then \(\mathbb E[Y \mid X] = f^\star(X)\), the definition of \(f^\star\). (Square-integrability makes every expectation finite; Cauchy-Schwarz for the cross term.) Hence \(R(f) - R(f^\star) = \mathbb E[(f^\star(X) - f(X))^2] \ge 0\), with equality only when \(f(X) = f^\star(X)\) with probability one. \(\blacksquare\)

The fact that does the work: a residual against the conditional mean, \(Y - f^\star(X)\), is orthogonal to every function of \(X\). It is the same first move as week 1’s bias-variance decomposition, and the cross term dies for the same reason.

Common errors: taking \(f^\star(X)\) outside an expectation without conditioning first; writing \(\mathbb E[Y - f^\star(X)] = 0\), which is true but not enough (the cross term needs the conditional version, \(\mathbb E[Y - f^\star(X) \mid X] = 0\), because \(g(X)\) is random); assuming \(f\) is linear. The theorem needs no linearity, which is the point of it.

Exercise 2 (omitted-variable bias). Two predictors matter: \(\mathbf y = \beta_1 \mathbf x_1 + \beta_2 \mathbf x_2 + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\), all vectors in \(\mathbb R^n\) and centered (the intercept partialled out, as in the lecture). What is fixed: the inputs \(\mathbf x_1\) and \(\mathbf x_2\), which we condition on. What is random: the noise, with \(\mathbb E\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon = \mathbf 0\), and through it \(\mathbf y\). Somebody regresses \(\mathbf y\) on \(\mathbf x_1\) alone and reports \(\hat\beta_{\text{short}} = \mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf y / \mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf x_1\), random through \(\mathbf y\). Show that \[ \mathbb E\big[\hat\beta_{\text{short}} \mid \mathbf x_1, \mathbf x_2\big] = \beta_1 + \beta_2\,\hat\delta, \qquad \hat\delta = \frac{\mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf x_2}{\mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf x_1}, \] say in words what \(\hat\delta\) is, and say why it carries no randomness. When is the short regression unbiased for \(\beta_1\)?

NoteHint 1

Substitute the model for \(\mathbf y\) inside \(\mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf y\) and split the fraction into three terms.

NoteHint 2

Two of the three terms are numbers once the inputs are fixed. Only the noise term is random: what is its expectation given the inputs?

TipReveal solution

Hint 1: \(\mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf y = \beta_1\,\mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf x_1 + \beta_2\,\mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf x_2 + \mathbf x_1^\top\boldsymbol\varepsilon\), so \[ \hat\beta_{\text{short}} = \beta_1 + \beta_2\,\hat\delta + \frac{\mathbf x_1^\top\boldsymbol\varepsilon}{\mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf x_1}. \] Hint 2: conditioning on the inputs, the last term has expectation \(\mathbf x_1^\top\,\mathbb E[\boldsymbol\varepsilon \mid \mathbf x_1, \mathbf x_2] / \mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf x_1 = 0\); the noise is mean zero given the inputs, and this is the one place the randomness enters. \(\blacksquare\)

\(\hat\delta\) is the least-squares slope of \(\mathbf x_2\) on \(\mathbf x_1\) in these inputs (through the origin, which for centered vectors is the slope with an intercept): units of \(x_2\) per unit of \(x_1\), a regression slope and not a correlation. It is a function of the fixed inputs only, so once they are given it is a number; the hat records that it is computed from data, not that it is random (contrast \(\hat\beta_{\text{short}}\), which is random through \(\mathbf y\)). The bias \(\beta_2\hat\delta\) is zero when the left-out variable does not matter (\(\beta_2 = 0\)) or is orthogonal to the kept one (\(\hat\delta = 0\), uncorrelated after centering); otherwise it has either sign and can be larger than \(\beta_1\) itself.

Common errors: using \(\mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf x_2\) for raw vectors when an intercept is fitted (the formula is then about the wrong regression); reading \(\hat\delta\) as a correlation; “the bias is \(\beta_2\)”, forgetting \(\hat\delta\); treating \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\) as zero rather than mean zero (the expectation is needed here; the exact in-sample version is problem 2); writing \(\mathbb E\,\hat\beta_{\text{short}}\) without saying what is being averaged over (the noise, with the inputs held fixed).

Exercise 3 (partialling-out theorem). The lecture proved this one. Close the slides and prove it again. \(\mathbf X = [\mathbf X_1 \;\; \mathbf x_2]\) has full column rank, with the intercept column inside \(\mathbf X_1\); \(\mathbf M_1 = \mathbf I - \mathbf X_1(\mathbf X_1^\top\mathbf X_1)^{-1}\mathbf X_1^\top\) and \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2 = \mathbf M_1\mathbf x_2\). You may use that \(\mathbf M_1\) is symmetric, that \(\mathbf M_1^2 = \mathbf M_1\) and that \(\mathbf M_1\mathbf X_1 = \mathbf 0\) (optional problem 4 proves all three). Show that the coefficient on \(\mathbf x_2\) in the least-squares regression of \(\mathbf y\) on \(\mathbf X\) is \[ \hat\beta_2 = \frac{\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top \mathbf y}{\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top \tilde{\mathbf x}_2}, \] and that regressing \(\tilde{\mathbf y} = \mathbf M_1\mathbf y\) on \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2\) gives the same number. Where is full column rank used?

NoteHint 1

Start from the full regression and its normal equations: \(\mathbf y = \mathbf X_1\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_1 + \mathbf x_2\hat\beta_2 + \mathbf r\), with \(\mathbf X_1^\top\mathbf r = \mathbf 0\) and \(\mathbf x_2^\top\mathbf r = 0\). Which matrix removes the \(\mathbf X_1\) term?

NoteHint 2

Multiply through by \(\mathbf M_1\) and say what happens to each of the three terms on the right. For the last one, write \(\mathbf M_1\mathbf r = \mathbf r - \mathbf X_1(\mathbf X_1^\top\mathbf X_1)^{-1}\mathbf X_1^\top\mathbf r\).

NoteHint 3

You now have \(\mathbf M_1\mathbf y = \tilde{\mathbf x}_2\,\hat\beta_2 + \mathbf r\): a vector equation with one unknown number. Multiply by a row vector that turns it into a scalar equation in which the coefficient of \(\hat\beta_2\) is positive.

NoteHint 4

After multiplying by \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top = \mathbf x_2^\top\mathbf M_1\), two things remain: the term \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\mathbf r\), and an extra \(\mathbf M_1\) on the left side. Each goes away by one of the properties of \(\mathbf M_1\) you were given.

TipReveal solution

The lecture’s six lines. Hint 1 is the start. Hint 2: \(\mathbf M_1\mathbf y = \mathbf M_1\mathbf X_1\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_1 + \mathbf M_1\mathbf x_2\hat\beta_2 + \mathbf M_1\mathbf r\), where \(\mathbf M_1\mathbf X_1 = \mathbf 0\), \(\mathbf M_1\mathbf x_2 = \tilde{\mathbf x}_2\) by definition, and \(\mathbf M_1\mathbf r = \mathbf r\) because \(\mathbf X_1^\top\mathbf r = \mathbf 0\) (the residual is already in the orthogonal complement of \(\mathrm{col}(\mathbf X_1)\)). So \[ \mathbf M_1\mathbf y = \tilde{\mathbf x}_2\,\hat\beta_2 + \mathbf r. \] Hint 3: multiply by \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\): \[ \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\mathbf M_1\mathbf y = \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\tilde{\mathbf x}_2\,\hat\beta_2 + \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\mathbf r. \] Hint 4, in two steps. Symmetry moves \(\mathbf M_1\) across an inner product: \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\mathbf r = \mathbf x_2^\top\mathbf M_1\mathbf r = \mathbf x_2^\top\mathbf r = 0\), the last normal equation. Idempotence removes the extra \(\mathbf M_1\): \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\mathbf M_1\mathbf y = \mathbf x_2^\top\mathbf M_1\mathbf M_1\mathbf y = \mathbf x_2^\top\mathbf M_1\mathbf y = \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\mathbf y\). Hence \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\mathbf y = \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\tilde{\mathbf x}_2\,\hat\beta_2\). Full column rank is used twice: it makes \(\mathbf X_1^\top\mathbf X_1\) invertible, so that \(\mathbf M_1\) is defined, and it makes \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2 \ne \mathbf 0\) (otherwise \(\mathbf x_2 = \mathbf X_1(\mathbf X_1^\top\mathbf X_1)^{-1}\mathbf X_1^\top\mathbf x_2\) would be a combination of the columns of \(\mathbf X_1\)), so \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\tilde{\mathbf x}_2 > 0\) and we may divide. \(\blacksquare\)

Residual on residual: the slope through the origin of \(\tilde{\mathbf y}\) on \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2\) is \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\tilde{\mathbf y}/\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\tilde{\mathbf x}_2\), and \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\tilde{\mathbf y} = \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\mathbf M_1\mathbf y = \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\mathbf y\) by the idempotence step again. (No intercept is needed in that regression: the intercept column is inside \(\mathbf X_1\), so both residuals already sum to zero.)

Common errors: “\(\mathbf M_1\mathbf x_2 = \mathbf 0\)” (true only for the columns of \(\mathbf X_1\)); asserting \(\mathbf M_1\mathbf r = \mathbf r\) without saying which normal equations give it; using \(\mathbf M_1\mathbf y = \mathbf y\); dividing by \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\tilde{\mathbf x}_2\) without saying why it is not zero; writing a ratio of vectors.

Problems

Problem 1 (the constant predictor). The setting of exercise 1.

  1. Take \(f\) to be a constant \(c\). Which \(c\) minimizes \(R(c)\), and what is \(R(c)\) at the minimum? Express the answer in terms of \(R(f^\star)\) and \(\mathrm{Var}\big(f^\star(X)\big)\).

  2. Deduce that \(\mathrm{Var}(Y) = R(f^\star) + \mathrm{Var}\big(f^\star(X)\big)\), and write both terms on the right as conditional moments of \(Y\) given \(X\). Which identity from ST102 is this?

  3. Week 1 scored methods by squared error on held-out data. Imagine two oracle entries: one predicts \(\mathbb E Y\) for everyone, the other is \(f^\star\). What is the gap between their risks? On an actual leaderboard the constant entry uses the training mean \(\bar y\) and the best entry is some fitted \(\hat f\); give two reasons the observed gap need not equal that quantity. Can any method’s held-out squared error be below \(R(f^\star)\) on average?

NoteHint 1
  1. Apply the theorem with \(f = c\), then add and subtract \(\mathbb E f^\star(X)\) inside the remaining square: exercise 1’s move again, with nothing left to condition on.
NoteHint 2
  1. \(R(\mathbb E Y)\) is a variance you know. (c) Apply the theorem to a fitted \(\hat f\) conditionally on the training data.
TipReveal answer

  1. By the theorem, \(R(c) = R(f^\star) + \mathbb E[(f^\star(X) - c)^2]\), and \(\mathbb E[(f^\star(X) - c)^2] = \mathrm{Var}(f^\star(X)) + (\mathbb E f^\star(X) - c)^2\) (hint 1: the cross term is zero because \(f^\star(X) - \mathbb E f^\star(X)\) has mean zero). So the minimizer is \(c^\star = \mathbb E f^\star(X) = \mathbb E Y\), by the tower property, and \(R(c^\star) = R(f^\star) + \mathrm{Var}(f^\star(X))\).

  2. \(R(\mathbb E Y) = \mathbb E[(Y - \mathbb E Y)^2] = \mathrm{Var}(Y)\), so \(\mathrm{Var}(Y) = R(f^\star) + \mathrm{Var}(f^\star(X))\). Conditioning on \(X\) inside \(R(f^\star)\) gives \(R(f^\star) = \mathbb E\big[\mathbb E[(Y - \mathbb E[Y \mid X])^2 \mid X]\big] = \mathbb E[\mathrm{Var}(Y \mid X)]\), and \(\mathrm{Var}(f^\star(X)) = \mathrm{Var}(\mathbb E[Y \mid X])\). This is the law of total variance, \(\mathrm{Var}(Y) = \mathbb E[\mathrm{Var}(Y \mid X)] + \mathrm{Var}(\mathbb E[Y \mid X])\): the optimal model theorem evaluated at the best constant.

  3. \(R(\mathbb E Y) - R(f^\star) = \mathrm{Var}(f^\star(X))\) by (b): the part of the variance of \(Y\) that is predictable from \(X\) at all. Equivalently \(1 - R(f^\star)/\mathrm{Var}(Y)\) is the ceiling on the population \(R^2\) of every method, a property of the joint distribution of \((X, Y)\) and not of any method. An observed gap differs from it for three reasons: the constant entry is \(\bar y\), not \(\mathbb E Y\), and \(R(\bar y) = \mathrm{Var}(Y) + (\bar y - \mathbb E Y)^2\), an extra term of order \(\mathrm{Var}(Y)/n\) on average; the best entry is a fitted \(\hat f\), not \(f^\star\), and its risk exceeds \(R(f^\star)\) by its own excess risk \(\mathbb E[(f^\star(X) - \hat f(X))^2]\), which reduces the gap, other things equal; and both scores are averages over a finite test set, which is what the error bars on week 1’s leaderboard were for. No: for a fitted \(\hat f\) and a fresh pair \((X, Y)\) independent of the training data, the theorem applied conditionally on the training data gives \(\mathbb E[(Y - \hat f(X))^2] = R(f^\star) + \mathbb E[(f^\star(X) - \hat f(X))^2] \ge R(f^\star)\); a score below \(R(f^\star)\) is test-set noise.

Common errors: differentiating \(\mathbb E[(Y - c)^2]\) in \(c\) directly (correct, but it misses that this is the theorem again); \(\mathrm{Var}(Y) = R(f^\star)\), forgetting the predictable part; in (c), “a better method could beat \(R(f^\star)\)”: nothing can, on average, by the theorem.

Problem 2 (leaving a variable out, exactly). The setting of exercise 2, but forget the truth: let \(\hat\beta_1\) and \(\hat\beta_2\) be the least-squares coefficients from the long regression of \(\mathbf y\) on both \(\mathbf x_1\) and \(\mathbf x_2\), with residual \(\mathbf r\), so that \(\mathbf y = \hat\beta_1\mathbf x_1 + \hat\beta_2\mathbf x_2 + \mathbf r\).

  1. Show that \(\hat\beta_{\text{short}} = \hat\beta_1 + \hat\beta_2\,\hat\delta\) exactly, with no expectation. Which fact about \(\mathbf r\) did you use?

  2. Numbers: \(\mathbf x_1 = (-1, 0, 1)\), \(\mathbf x_2 = (-2, 1, 1)\), \(\beta_1 = 1\), \(\beta_2 = 2\). Compute \(\hat\delta\), and what the short regression estimates on average.

  3. Suppose \(\mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf x_2 = 0\). What happens to the coefficient on \(\mathbf x_1\) when \(\mathbf x_2\) is dropped? Reconcile your answer with the partialling-out theorem: what is the residual of \(\mathbf x_1\) after regressing it on \(\mathbf x_2\)?

NoteHint 1
  1. Multiply the fitted equation \(\mathbf y = \hat\beta_1\mathbf x_1 + \hat\beta_2\mathbf x_2 + \mathbf r\) by \(\mathbf x_1^\top\), as in exercise 2, and look at the last term.
NoteHint 2
  1. Which fact about a least-squares residual makes that last term vanish exactly? It is the sample version of the fact that killed the cross term in exercise 1. (c) In the theorem, partial \(\mathbf x_2\) out of \(\mathbf x_1\): what is left of \(\mathbf x_1\) when the two are orthogonal?
TipReveal answer

  1. Multiplying by \(\mathbf x_1^\top\) and dividing by \(\mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf x_1\): \(\hat\beta_{\text{short}} = \hat\beta_1 + \hat\beta_2\hat\delta + \mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf r/\mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf x_1\), and \(\mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf r = 0\) by the normal equations. The fact: a least-squares residual is orthogonal to every column it was fitted on, the sample twin of exercise 1’s population fact (the residual \(Y - f^\star(X)\) is orthogonal to every function of \(X\)). The same algebra as exercise 2 with the fitted coefficients in place of the truth and the residual in place of the noise, except that the last term is exactly zero rather than zero on average: the noise is not orthogonal to \(\mathbf x_1\) in the sample, only mean zero given the inputs, which is why exercise 2 needs an expectation and this problem does not. Coding part 3 checks the identity on real data to every printed digit.

  2. Both vectors are centered, so the formula applies as written. \(\mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf x_2 = 2 + 0 + 1 = 3\) and \(\mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf x_1 = 2\), so \(\hat\delta = 1.5\) and \(\mathbb E\,\hat\beta_{\text{short}} = 1 + 2 \times 1.5 = 4\): four times the coefficient it was meant to estimate.

  3. \(\hat\delta = 0\), so \(\hat\beta_{\text{short}} = \hat\beta_1\): dropping an orthogonal column does not move the coefficient, and by exercise 2 the short regression is then unbiased. From the theorem: partial \(\mathbf x_2\) out of \(\mathbf x_1\); the residual is \(\mathbf x_1 - \mathbf x_2\,(\mathbf x_2^\top\mathbf x_1)/(\mathbf x_2^\top\mathbf x_2) = \mathbf x_1\) when the two are orthogonal, so the long coefficient on \(\mathbf x_1\) is \(\mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf y/\mathbf x_1^\top\mathbf x_1\), which is the short one. On the standard error: the two regressions estimate the noise variance from different residuals, and the short one’s residual still contains \(\hat\beta_2\mathbf x_2\), so the reported standard error usually grows when a relevant orthogonal column is dropped even though the coefficient does not move; the details are for the validation week.

Common errors: raw vectors in place of centered ones; believing (a) needs an expectation (it is an identity of the data); in (c), “the coefficient is the same, so the two regressions are the same” (the residuals differ).

Problem 3 (normal equations, and centering). Simple regression, from the lecture’s “nice geometry” slide. The training error is \(\hat R_n(\alpha, \beta) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \alpha - \beta x_i)^2\).

  1. Compute \(\partial \hat R_n/\partial\alpha\) and \(\partial \hat R_n/\partial\beta\), set both to zero, and show that the residuals \(r_i = y_i - \hat\alpha - \hat\beta x_i\) satisfy \(\sum_i r_i = 0\) and \(\sum_i x_i r_i = 0\). Write the same two equations as \(\mathbf X^\top \mathbf r = \mathbf 0\) with \(\mathbf X = [\mathbf 1 \;\; \mathbf x]\).

  2. Solve them: show \(\hat\beta = \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y) \big/ \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2\) and \(\hat\alpha = \bar y - \hat\beta \bar x\).

  3. In the partialling-out theorem take \(\mathbf X_1 = \mathbf 1\), the column of ones, and \(\mathbf x_2 = \mathbf x\). What is \(\mathbf M_1 \mathbf x\)? Conclude that the slope of \(\mathbf y\) on \(\mathbf x\) in a regression with an intercept is the slope through the origin of the centered \(\mathbf y\) on the centered \(\mathbf x\), and check that this is (b).

NoteHint 1
  1. Differentiate one term of the sum first. (b) Use the first equation to eliminate \(\hat\alpha\) from the second.
NoteHint 2
  1. \(\mathbf H_1 = \mathbf 1(\mathbf 1^\top\mathbf 1)^{-1}\mathbf 1^\top\): what is \(\mathbf 1^\top\mathbf 1\), and what does \(\mathbf H_1\) do to a vector?
TipReveal answer

  1. \(\partial \hat R_n/\partial\alpha = -\frac2n\sum_i (y_i - \alpha - \beta x_i)\) and \(\partial \hat R_n/\partial\beta = -\frac2n\sum_i x_i (y_i - \alpha - \beta x_i)\). Setting both to zero at \((\hat\alpha, \hat\beta)\) gives \(\sum_i r_i = 0\) and \(\sum_i x_i r_i = 0\): the two rows of \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf r = \mathbf 0\), one per column of \(\mathbf X\). Since \(\bar r = 0\) and \(\sum x_i r_i = 0\), the sample correlation of \(x\) and \(r\) is zero: no linear signal is left in the residuals, which is all least squares promises.

  2. The first equation gives \(\hat\alpha = \bar y - \hat\beta \bar x\). Substitute into the second: \(\sum_i x_i (y_i - \bar y - \hat\beta(x_i - \bar x)) = 0\), and because \(\sum_i (y_i - \bar y) = 0 = \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)\) you may replace \(x_i\) by \(x_i - \bar x\) in the sum, giving \(\hat\beta = \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y) / \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2\).

  3. \(\mathbf H_1 = \mathbf 1(\mathbf 1^\top\mathbf 1)^{-1}\mathbf 1^\top = \frac1n \mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top\), so \(\mathbf H_1\mathbf x = \bar x\mathbf 1\) and \(\mathbf M_1\mathbf x = \mathbf x - \bar x\mathbf 1\): partialling out the intercept is centering. The theorem gives \(\hat\beta = \tilde{\mathbf x}^\top\mathbf y/\tilde{\mathbf x}^\top\tilde{\mathbf x}\) with \(\tilde{\mathbf x} = \mathbf x - \bar x\mathbf 1\), and since \(\tilde{\mathbf x}^\top \mathbf y = \tilde{\mathbf x}^\top(\mathbf y - \bar y\mathbf 1)\) (the centered \(\tilde{\mathbf x}\) sums to zero) this is \(\sum (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)/\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2\), part (b). The same step is why exercise 2 and problem 2 use centered vectors whenever an intercept is fitted.

Common errors: the chain rule’s \(-1\) (harmless here); in (b), dividing by \(\sum x_i^2\) instead of \(\sum (x_i - \bar x)^2\).

Problem 4 (the hat matrix). \(\mathbf X\) is \(n \times q\) with full column rank (\(q = p + 1\) columns, the intercept counted), \(\mathbf H = \mathbf X(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\) and \(\mathbf M = \mathbf I - \mathbf H\).

  1. Show \(\mathbf H^2 = \mathbf H\) and \(\mathbf H^\top = \mathbf H\), and compute \(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf H)\).

  2. Show \(\mathbf M^2 = \mathbf M\), \(\mathbf M^\top = \mathbf M\) and \(\mathbf M \mathbf X = \mathbf 0\). In one sentence each: what is \(\mathbf H\mathbf y\), and what is \(\mathbf M\mathbf y\)?

  3. Use (b) to show that the residual vector \(\mathbf r = \mathbf M\mathbf y\) satisfies \(\mathbf X^\top \mathbf r = \mathbf 0\) (the normal equations again, from the geometry instead of the calculus). Where did full column rank get used?

NoteHint 1
  1. For the trace, \(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf A\mathbf B) = \mathrm{tr}(\mathbf B\mathbf A)\) with \(\mathbf A = \mathbf X\).
NoteHint 2
  1. Write \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf r = \mathbf X^\top\mathbf M\mathbf y\) and use \(\mathbf M^\top = \mathbf M\) to move \(\mathbf M\) onto \(\mathbf X\).
TipReveal answer

  1. \(\mathbf H^2 = \mathbf X(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top = \mathbf H\), the middle \((\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\) cancelling; \(\mathbf H^\top = \mathbf H\) because \((\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\) is symmetric; \(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf H) = \mathrm{tr}\big((\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\big) = \mathrm{tr}(\mathbf I_q) = q\) by the cyclic property \(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf A\mathbf B) = \mathrm{tr}(\mathbf B\mathbf A)\). So the hat matrix has trace \(p + 1\) with the intercept counted: the number of coefficients. (The validation week turns this trace into degrees of freedom.)

  2. \(\mathbf M^2 = \mathbf I - 2\mathbf H + \mathbf H^2 = \mathbf I - \mathbf H = \mathbf M\); symmetric because \(\mathbf H\) is; \(\mathbf M\mathbf X = \mathbf X - \mathbf X(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X = \mathbf X - \mathbf X = \mathbf 0\). \(\mathbf H\mathbf y\) is the fitted vector \(\hat{\mathbf y}\), the point of \(\mathrm{col}(\mathbf X)\) closest to \(\mathbf y\); \(\mathbf M\mathbf y\) is the residual vector, what is left of \(\mathbf y\) after \(\mathrm{col}(\mathbf X)\) has explained what it can.

  3. \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf r = \mathbf X^\top\mathbf M\mathbf y = (\mathbf M\mathbf X)^\top\mathbf y = \mathbf 0\), using \(\mathbf M^\top = \mathbf M\) and \(\mathbf M\mathbf X = \mathbf 0\). Full column rank is what makes \(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X\) invertible, so that \(\mathbf H\) is defined at all; without it the projection onto \(\mathrm{col}(\mathbf X)\) still exists (a pseudoinverse gives it) but the coefficients are not unique, which is the NA that lm prints when one column is a copy of the others.

Common errors: \(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf H) = n\) (confusing \(\mathbf H\) with the identity); claiming \(\mathbf H = \mathbf I\) because \(\mathbf H^2 = \mathbf H\).

Problem 5 (a zero slope with a predictable outcome). Let \(X\) take the values \(-1, 0, 1\) with probability \(1/3\) each, let \(\varepsilon\) be independent of \(X\) and take the values \(-1, 1\) with probability \(1/2\) each, and let \(Y = X^2 + \varepsilon\).

  1. Find \(f^\star(x)\) under squared loss, and its risk \(R(f^\star)\).

  2. Find the best linear predictor \(a + bX\), the minimizer of \(\mathbb E[(Y - a - bX)^2]\), and its risk. Compute \(\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)\).

  3. What does the example say about using correlation to decide whether \(X\) predicts \(Y\)? Which result from the lecture gives the excess risk of the best line, and what is it here?

NoteHint 1
  1. \(\mathbb E[Y \mid X = x]\) is \(x^2\) plus the mean of the noise.
NoteHint 2
  1. The best line’s slope is \(\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)/\mathrm{Var}(X)\); compute \(\mathbb E X^3\). Its risk is then the variance of something.
TipReveal answer

  1. \(f^\star(x) = \mathbb E[Y \mid X = x] = x^2\), because \(\varepsilon\) is independent of \(X\) with mean zero; \(R(f^\star) = \mathbb E\,\varepsilon^2 = 1\).

  2. \(\mathbb E X = 0\) and \(\mathbb E X^3 = 0\), so \(\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathrm{Cov}(X, X^2) = \mathbb E X^3 - \mathbb E X\,\mathbb E X^2 = 0\): the slope is \(b = 0\) and the intercept is \(a = \mathbb E Y = \mathbb E X^2 = 2/3\). Its risk is \(\mathbb E[(Y - 2/3)^2] = \mathrm{Var}(Y) = \mathrm{Var}(X^2) + \mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon) = (\mathbb E X^4 - (\mathbb E X^2)^2) + 1 = (2/3 - 4/9) + 1 = 11/9\).

  3. Zero correlation and a zero slope, yet \(Y\) is predictable from \(X\) up to the noise: correlation detects linear signal only. The optimal model theorem gives the excess risk of any predictor as \(\mathbb E[(f^\star(X) - f(X))^2]\); for the best line that is \(\mathbb E[(X^2 - 2/3)^2] = \mathrm{Var}(X^2) = 2/9\), which is \(11/9 - 1\). The same lesson as coding part 4’s x2, in a case small enough to do by hand.

Common errors: concluding from \(\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = 0\) that \(X\) carries no information about \(Y\); computing \(\mathrm{Var}(Y)\) as \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon)\) alone.

Problem 6 (why collinearity makes a coefficient uncertain). Fixed design: \(\mathbf y = \mathbf X_1\boldsymbol\beta_1 + \mathbf x_2\beta_2 + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\) with \(\mathbb E\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon = \mathbf 0\) and \(\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\mathbf I\), full column rank, and \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2 = \mathbf M_1\mathbf x_2\) as in the lecture.

  1. Use the partialling-out theorem to show that \(\hat\beta_2 = \beta_2 + \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\boldsymbol\varepsilon / \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\tilde{\mathbf x}_2\). Which two facts about \(\mathbf M_1\) do you need?

  2. Find \(\mathbb E\,\hat\beta_2\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\beta_2)\).

  3. What happens as \(\mathbf x_2\) becomes almost a linear combination of the columns of \(\mathbf X_1\)? Compare two designs with \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\tilde{\mathbf x}_2 = 100\) and \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\tilde{\mathbf x}_2 = 4\), with the same \(\sigma^2\).

NoteHint 1
  1. The theorem gives \(\hat\beta_2 = \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\mathbf y/\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\tilde{\mathbf x}_2\); substitute the model for \(\mathbf y\).
NoteHint 2
  1. You need \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\mathbf X_1 = \mathbf 0\) and \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\mathbf x_2 = \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\tilde{\mathbf x}_2\): which property of \(\mathbf M_1\) gives each? (b) For the variance of a ratio with a fixed denominator, square the denominator.
TipReveal answer

  1. By the theorem, \(\hat\beta_2 = \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\mathbf y / \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\tilde{\mathbf x}_2\). Substitute the model for \(\mathbf y\): \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\mathbf X_1 = \mathbf x_2^\top\mathbf M_1\mathbf X_1 = \mathbf 0\) (\(\mathbf M_1\) is symmetric and \(\mathbf M_1\mathbf X_1 = \mathbf 0\)), and \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\mathbf x_2 = \mathbf x_2^\top\mathbf M_1\mathbf x_2 = \mathbf x_2^\top\mathbf M_1\mathbf M_1\mathbf x_2 = \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\tilde{\mathbf x}_2\) (\(\mathbf M_1\) is idempotent). So \(\hat\beta_2 = \beta_2 + \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\boldsymbol\varepsilon / \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\tilde{\mathbf x}_2\): the fitted coefficient is the true one plus the noise projected onto \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2\).

  2. \(\mathbb E\,\hat\beta_2 = \beta_2\), and \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\beta_2) = \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top(\sigma^2\mathbf I)\tilde{\mathbf x}_2 / (\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\tilde{\mathbf x}_2)^2 = \sigma^2 / \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top\tilde{\mathbf x}_2\).

  3. The denominator is the squared length of what is left of \(\mathbf x_2\) after \(\mathbf X_1\) has explained what it can. As \(\mathbf x_2\) approaches \(\mathrm{col}(\mathbf X_1)\) that length goes to zero and the variance blows up; at zero the coefficient is not identified, which is the NA from the lecture. With \(100\) against \(4\) the variance ratio is \(25\): a standard deviation five times larger in the second design. Collinearity does not bias the coefficient; it makes it uncertain, and the picture in the lecture says why: a short \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2\) is a short baseline for a slope.

Common errors: writing \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\beta_2) = \sigma^2/\mathbf x_2^\top\mathbf x_2\) (the raw column instead of the residual), which is the variance in the regression on \(\mathbf x_2\) alone; forgetting the square in the denominator when taking the variance of the ratio.

Question 2. How do we interpret diagnostic plots?

TipReveal answer

Generally looking for patterns in residuals, normality of their distribution (diagonal line in QQ plot), constant variance (homoscedasticity), and influential points (high-leverage outliers). Two cautions from the lecture: a correctly specified conditional mean gives \(\mathbb E[\text{residual} \mid X] \approx 0\), but says nothing about the spread, so a fan shape is a separate matter (constant variance is its own assumption); and a pattern in a finite noisy sample is evidence that the class of functions may be missing something, not a proof that it is.

Question 3. Question 4. In one sentence, what is the coefficient on lgdp in long the slope of? Your neighbor regressed the residual of lifeExp (on lpop and continent) on lgdp_tilde and got the same number. Why is that not a coincidence?

TipReveal answer

The slope of lifeExp on the part of lgdp not linearly explained by lpop, the continent indicators and the intercept. Residual on residual gives the same slope because \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top \mathbf M_1 \mathbf y = \tilde{\mathbf x}_2^\top \mathbf y\): \(\mathbf M_1\) is symmetric and idempotent, so it can be moved onto \(\tilde{\mathbf x}_2\), where it changes nothing (lines 5 to 6 of the lecture’s proof). If the numbers disagree, the residual came from the wrong regression (lifeExp instead of lgdp on the left), the two fits used different rows, or a column was left out of the auxiliary regression. Dropping the explicit intercept with - 1 does not change the answer here: R then codes every continent with its own indicator, and together they span the constant column.

Question 5. Did leaving out lpop move the coefficient on lgdp by much, and in which direction? Exercise 2 names two things the size of the move depends on; which of them is small here? Is the short coefficient “wrong”?

TipReveal answer

On these data the move is tiny, about \(0.025\) years per unit of \(\log_{10}\) GDP, and downward: 10.69 in the long regression, 10.66 in the short one.              x1 = x1,

Then plot the residuals against x2 (augment(my_model) from broom gives a data frame with a .resid column). Would part 2’s diagnostics have caught this?

augment(my_model) |>
  ggplot(aes(x2, .resid)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.5) +
  geom_smooth(se = FALSE)
TipReveal answer

Least squares fits the best linear approximation. For \(X \sim U(0, 2)\), \(\mathrm{Cov}(X, X^2)/\mathrm{Var}(X) = (2 - 1 \cdot 4/3)/(1/3) = 2\), so the population slope of \(2X - X^2\) on \(X\) is \(2 - 2 = 0\); the predictors are independent, so this is also the multiple-regression coefficient, and the fitted coefficient is near zero with a tight interval (at \(n = 400\) the estimate is usually within \(\pm 0.15\) of zero and the interval about \(\pm 0.2\) wide; at \(n = 100\) the same picture, noisier). The derivative \(2 - 2x_2\) is \(+2\) at one end and \(-2\) at the other; the linear fit averages it away. x3 is simply irrelevant. The residuals against x2 show an inverted U: the class of planes does not contain \(f\), and a one-predictor residual plot does show it here, because the missing piece is a function of one input. A coefficient near zero with a tight interval is evidence of no linear signal, not of no effect.

Challenge: can you find some potential dangers of using regression models, where the model is wrong in some important way and our diagnostic methods fail to show us the problem? (A missing piece that depends on two inputs at once is a good place to start.)

Challenge: what are some strengths or robustness properties of regression models that we should appreciate and trust in the long run? i.e. are there ways the model can be wrong but our conclusions from it will still (usually?) be good enough to use anyway?

TipReveal answer

Danger: put an interaction or a product like \(x_1 x_2\) into f; the coefficients on x1 and x2 are then slopes of a plane through a curved surface, and the one-predictor residual plots can look clean while the model is badly wrong along a diagonal. Strengths: when the truth is close to linear over the range of the data and \(n\) is large relative to \(p\), the fitted plane is close to the best plane and its predictions inside that range are hard to beat; and even when the truth is curved there is a best plane, the one with the smallest risk among planes (the optimal model theorem with the minimization restricted to planes), and least squares estimates it. Sample least squares minimizes training error. Estimating the coefficients from finite data can leave its population risk above that of the best plane, by an amount the validation week measures.
